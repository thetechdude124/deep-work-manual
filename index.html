Deep Work for Open-Ended Projects

An instruction manual for college students

The objective of this manual is to provide detailed instructions and information on how college students (particularly those in STEM-related fields) can independently formulate, plan, and successfully execute open-ended personal projects (such as programming an application, improving mathematical ability, writing a book, painting, etc.) without clear deadlines. We’ve split up these instructions into four concrete chunks that you can find below.

Before we begin, it is critical to define deep work. Cal Newport popularized this term in his seminal work Deep Work, and defined it as

“Professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit. These efforts create new value, improve your skill, and are hard to replicate.”

Conversely, shallow work is defined as

“Non-cognitively demanding, logistical-style tasks, often performed while distracted. These efforts tend to not create new value in the world and are easy to replicate.”

Shallow work still has to get done (email, setup, scheduling). It just shouldn’t take the time you set aside for deep work, so give it your low-energy hours (Section 2.2).

Flow state, a related concept coined by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, describes a state where one is

“being completely involved in an activity for its own sake. The ego falls away. Time flies. Every action, movement, and thought follows inevitably from the previous one, like playing jazz. Your whole being is involved, and you're using your skills to the utmost.”
Why this works

Research has empirically supported the importance of deep work and flow state, showing that prolonged, deliberate work that is effortful and improvement-oriented also strongly correlates with expert performance (while task switching typically hampers performance and concentration). Flow state in particular has also been strongly correlated with overall well-being and desirable personal and work outcomes.

Consequently, this guide will be centred around increasing the likelihood that one consistently achieves these states in pursuit of a larger open-ended project to maximize efficiency, effectiveness, and output per unit of time.

What you’ll need

  • One place to write goals down: a notebook, a paper planner, or an app (see Section 1.1). The templates at the end of this manual also work.
  • A calendar (paper, Google Calendar, or Apple Calendar).
  • A timer (phone or kitchen timer).
  • Earbuds or headphones, if you work anywhere noisy.

1. Determining Project Structure

Rule of thumb: if you can’t check it off, it isn’t a goal yet. Every goal gets a timeframe and a finish line.

1.1 Establishing a goal-tracking platform

  1. Pick one place where every goal in this manual will live: your multi-session goals, your session goals, and your session logs from Section 4. Paper works (a notebook, a planner such as a Passion Planner, or the templates at the end of this manual). So does digital (Google Sheets, Notion, Todoist, or a notes app).
  2. Choose the one you’ll actually open every day. A system you abandon after a week is worse than a plain notebook you keep using.
  3. If you’re not sure, use the printed templates at the end of this manual for your first two weeks, then decide whether to switch.
  4. Keep it where you work. Sections 3 and 4 ask you to write things down before and after every session.

1.2 Multi-Session Goals: Setting a timeframe for work completion

  1. Set an understanding goal. Create estimates for understanding the project specification, mentally separating the project components, learning dependencies between work items, and any limitations that will steer your solution.
  2. Construct a timeframe for completing the understanding goal. Consider in what order you would prefer to understand the project components. Keep in mind that understanding a project component is a prerequisite to implementing it, and the order you complete work will reflect your order of understanding.
  3. Set output goals to cover the project components. Describe your goals with timeframes to naturally separate work items and establish a schedule of work, as well as providing a mechanism for checking your progress.

    Note: Expect your first estimates to be too optimistic. Section 4.3 is where you correct them.

Why this works

People consistently underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know similar tasks ran late before.

1.3 Single-Session Goals: Creating measurable tools for evaluating work sessions

  1. Break each week’s output goal into pieces that fit in one sitting (at most 90 minutes; see Section 3.3).
  2. Write each piece as an output someone else could check, not an activity.
    Session goalNot a session goal
    Prove lemma 2Study for the proof
    Draft 300 words of chapter 1Work on the book
    Sketch 3 thumbnails for the coverThink about the cover
  3. List the coming week’s session goals on your platform from 1.1. Each day, Section 3.1 pulls the next one off this list.
Why this works

Specific, challenging goals lead to better performance than vague “do your best” goals.

1.4 Covering both outcome expectations and learning objectives

  1. For each multi-session goal, write two lines. The outcome goal is what will exist when you’re done. The learning goal is what you’ll be able to do or understand that you can’t now.

    Example. Outcome: the login page works end to end. Learning: understand how session tokens are stored and checked.

  2. When the work is new to you, lead with the learning goal.
  3. Once you know how to do the work, switch the emphasis to the outcome goal.
Why this works

On complex tasks where you don’t have the skills yet, learning goals lead to better performance than outcome goals, which push people to rush for a result before they know how to get it.

Section 1 summary

Choose one tracking system, break the project into goals with timeframes and clear finish lines, and distinguish what you want to produce from what you need to learn.

2. Organizing Your Life Around the Project

Rule of thumb: block project time like a class you’re enrolled in, with a fixed day and a fixed start time.

2.1 Take inventory of your fixed commitments and block them

  1. Put every fixed commitment for a normal week on your calendar first: classes, work shifts, commuting, meals, exercise, clubs, and sleep. Budget 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night, the recommended range for adults 18 to 25.
  2. Count the open hours that are left. Plan to use fewer of them than you think you can (see Section 1.2 on optimistic estimates).
  3. Block project time as recurring calendar events with a specific start time, not “sometime Tuesday.” If you have a job, caretaking duties, or a long commute, build this from your real week, not a typical student schedule.

2.2 Assigning a reasonable amount of work to blocks that match your energy and level of attention

  1. Write down 2 to 3 times of day when you focus best.
  2. Put deep work (Section 3) in those peak blocks. Put shallow work (email, setup, admin) in your low-energy slots.
  3. Size each deep work block to the session length you pick in Section 3.3 (never more than 90 minutes without a break). Only assign as many session goals from Section 1.3 as you have blocks for.

Why this works

2.3 Building in recovery into your calendar and set your own checkpoints, since no one else will

  1. Keep one buffer block a week with nothing assigned to it. When a session gets missed, it moves there instead of pushing your whole schedule back.
    • Warning: If several sessions are missed, move only what fits into the buffer block; reschedule the rest instead of cramming them together.
  2. Schedule real time off from the project.
  3. Set a weekly checkpoint for yourself, on a fixed day, where you run the review in Section 4.3.
  4. Tell one person what your checkpoints are and show them your progress. The ideal person knows you well enough to tell you when you need rest, not only when you’re behind.
Why this works

Mentally switching off from work during your off time is linked to better recovery and well-being.

Tracking progress works better when you report it to someone else.

Section 2 summary

Schedule around your existing commitments and best focus times. Match goals to the blocks available, reserve a buffer, and protect recovery time and a weekly checkpoint.

3. Executing Daily Deep Work

Rule of thumb: write down the minimum goal and the first action before you start, and stop at 90 minutes.

3.1 Pre-session: Defining core targets for session and deliverables

Each step below fills in part of a session card (the blank template is in Section 5). An example card will pop up on the side (at the bottom on a phone) showing what that step looks like filled in.

  1. In section 1, we broke down the overarching project objectives into a series of weekly and daily objectives. Now, based on that, take the daily objective you planned, and take the first component of said objective. This should be moderately sized, something that is reasonably achievable in 1-2 hours at most. Use this as your session goal, and write one line on why this piece matters to the larger project.
    Try this

    Note: If 1-2 hours is more than you can focus for right now, pick a smaller piece and split the session into shorter blocks (for example 25-minute Pomodoro blocks with 5-minute breaks). Section 3.3 explains how to pick your length.

  2. Set both a minimum and stretch goal. Based on the session goal you chose in the previous section, think about the most rudimentary, realistic version of said goal you could achieve in the first 10-20 minutes (that, if completed, would still move you forward). Label this the minimum session goal, and the full 1-2 hour objective as the stretch goal.
    • Warning: The stretch goal is optional. Missing it does not mean you missed your minimum goal.
  3. Write both the minimum and maximum goals on the same medium you chose to use as described in section 1 for organization purposes. Place it in a location where it is constantly visible. This will be useful for staying on task in section 3.3. (The session card at the end of this manual has a slot for each.)
Try this

For those who find it hard to start working: write down the first concrete action that you can take to achieve your minimum goal. This will make the process have substantially less friction and make it easier for you to get started.

Why this works

deciding in advance exactly when and how you’ll act (an “implementation intention”) had a medium-to-large effect on actually reaching goals across 94 studies, and it helps most with getting started.

Why this works

Students who took fixed Pomodoro-style breaks reported less fatigue and distraction, and more concentration and motivation, than students who took breaks whenever they felt like it.

3.2 Environmental optimization: setting up your desk and local surroundings for maximum productivity

Side note

Note: different individuals suit different working environments, and the following advice may not universally apply to all who are reading these instructions. These are evidence-based best practices for productivity that tend to allow people to focus better and in a healthier manner, so treat them as mini-experiments that you can run and decide to adopt. Feel free to discard advice if you find it personally hampers your productivity.

  • Find a quiet place to begin your work session, one that has sufficiently low volume so that you’re not actively disturbed or distracted. If you can’t find such a location, try using noise-cancelling headphones or earbuds to mute some of the noise.
    Try this
    • If it’s particularly bad and you can’t focus, try wearing earbuds, and then headphones over the earbuds to block out most noises.
    • Some people focus worse in silence. If that’s you, try a moderate background hum instead, like a café.
  • Not every project happens at a computer. If you’re painting, playing an instrument, or working on paper, skip the screen and keyboard advice below and apply the same ideas to your own setup: a clear surface, good light, and a posture you can hold for the whole session.
  • If you have the ability to setup your work session (e.g. You’re working at home on your desk), then read the following subbullets. Otherwise, skip to the next section.
    • To make your setup optimized for long-term comfort, lift your monitor or screen to roughly eye level. Ensure that your elbows are at a 90 degree angle when typing, and that your chair has adequate back support to prevent being actively hunched over. You may need to raise your chair height or place your monitor on books to achieve these desired configurations.
    • Keep your pelvis upright. If your hips roll back, your spine slumps no matter how good the backrest is. Sit all the way back in the seat, and if your chair doesn’t hold your hips upright, try a firm cushion or a rolled towel behind your lower back for lumbar support.
    • If you don’t have a monitor and just have a laptop, try to place your laptop in an elevated position and use an external keyboard and mouse instead. If this isn’t possible, no worries (do not let perfect be the enemy of good): try and implement as much of the other advice as possible instead.
  • If you don’t have the ability to setup your work session (e.g. You’re working in public), read the following subbullets, in order.
    1. Find a place with adequate power outlets to ensure that laptop battery is not a concern.
    2. Try adjusting the seat or desk height if possible to obtain a 90 degree angle between your elbows and the desk. Again, if this is not possible, do not worry!
    3. Make sure the area you’re in is safe before you start working, and put in your earbuds/headphones!
Other things to try

Try these one at a time for a week each and keep what helps (Section 3.4).

  • Light. Work near a window or under bright light during the day.
  • Temperature. Aim for a room that feels slightly cool.
  • Clutter. Clear everything off your desk that the session doesn’t need, including your phone.
  • Plants. Put a plant in view.
  • Ceiling and color. These effects are smaller. In lab studies, high ceilings nudged people toward big-picture thinking and low ceilings toward detail work, and red backgrounds helped detail-oriented tasks while blue helped creative ones, though that color result has been disputed. Try these last.
Why this works

In one set of experiments, moderate ambient noise (around 70 dB) improved creative thinking compared to a quiet room, while loud noise (85 dB) hurt it.

Brighter white light made people feel more alert in most of the studies that tested it.

Office task performance peaked around 22 °C (about 72 °F) and dropped as rooms got warmer.

Extra objects in view compete for your brain’s attention, and even a phone notification you don’t answer disrupts focus.

Adding plants to bare offices improved concentration and measured productivity in three field experiments.

3.3 In-session: maintaining focus, handling getting stuck, dealing with distractions that occur

  1. First, decide on an optimal working duration for the session. This will be highly dependent on your prior continuous work capacity. For instance, if you struggle to focus even 25 minutes at a time, it is unwise to attempt a session for 1-2 hours continuously. Therefore, we advise the following. Take the longest consecutive time you’ve been able to consecutively focus (that’s still less than 90 minutes), and try and sit for that duration of time.
    • We recommend capping the session at 90 minutes due to various research (linked below) that suggest human focus cycles can follow roughly 90 minute cycles, and empirical work suggests stopping sessions around this length.
    • that demonstrates that human focus cycles tend to occur in 90 minute bouts, synced with ultradian cycles. For this reason, if you are attempting a longer session (e.g. 3 hours), we recommend you divide it into at most 90 minute chunks (feel free to make the duration low if you feel that you are unable to focus for that long), and then take short 10-20 minute breaks between each.
  2. Make sure that your notes with the minimum and stretch goals from section 3.1 is in front of you and visible. If you wrote down the immediate next step that you will take, read that again as well.
  3. Now, begin the work session with the task that’s written down. Ideally this should be with no music, or minimal non-distracting, non-lyrical music that will not drag your attention to it.
    Side note

    Note: if music is the thing that gets you to sit down at all, use it. A session with music beats no session. Try it both ways and compare your logs in Section 4.

  4. If you begin to feel distracted and urges that wish to pull you away from the work, take a second to close your eyes and take a full inhale and long, slow exhale. Then open your eyes and return to work. Before the session, write down on your session card what is most likely to pull you away and what you’ll do when it happens.
  5. If you get stuck on a problem, don’t leave the session. Write down exactly where you’re stuck in one sentence (there’s a slot for it on the session card), then drop to your minimum goal from Section 3.1 or to a different piece of the same task. Come back to the stuck part at the start of your next session.
    • Warning: “Don’t leave the session” means don’t abandon the task just because you are stuck; it does not mean skipping your scheduled breaks.
  6. Keep going until the session is done!
    • If your session consists of multiple work blocks of <= 90 minutes, at the end of each, take a small break for 10-20 minutes. Make sure this break is timed so that it doesn’t bleed into the next session and try and let your mind actively rest. Then, return to work.
Why this works

(evidence, linked below, shows that music with lyrics tends to hurt productivity while instrumental music can have neutral/slightly negative effects for very demanding tasks).

Software developers who picked their own music reported better mood and produced better work than without music, though music you like still hurts memory-heavy work like memorizing or close reading.

Evidence (linked below) shows that mindful breathing can help reduce mind-wandering symptoms as opposed to pure relaxation.

3.4 Post-session: taking good breaks, recording task completion

  1. Now that the session is over, reflect on what went well and didn’t go well. Was the work session effective? Did you find yourself able to achieve flow state (see the definition at the start of this manual)? When did time fly, and when did it drag? Were you distracted by tasks, thoughts, people, etc throughout? If so, what were the greatest sources of distraction? What do you understand now that you didn’t at the start (your learning goal from Section 1.4)?
  2. Based on this, see if there’s any variable you’d change before beginning your next work session. Productivity and deep work is an experiment, and you likely won’t arrive at the most optimal configuration for you the first time!
  3. Write your answers on the session card before you get up, including where you’ll pick up next session, then log the result as described in Section 4.2.

Section 3 summary

Start with a minimum goal, a stretch goal, and a concrete first action. Prepare a comfortable workspace, work in manageable blocks with breaks, and record what helped or distracted you.

4. Quantifying Productivity

Rule of thumb: log every session the moment it ends, review once a week, and change one thing at a time.

4.1 Define one measurable output before you begin every work session

  1. Your minimum goal from Section 3.1 is this output. Write it so that someone else could check whether you did it.
  2. For creative or exploratory work (a novel, a painting, open research), you often can’t know the output in advance. Make the measurable output about the process instead: “write 500 words,” “45 minutes of freewriting on chapter 3,” “3 color studies,” or “try 2 approaches to the proof.” You’re checking that the session happened and produced something, not that it produced the right thing.

4.2 Track your results immediately after every work session

  1. Before you get up, log five things: the date, your planned output, the result (done, partial, or not done), minutes of actual focused work, and one line on what helped or hurt.
  2. Keep notes to one line. A log that’s too tedious to fill out will stop getting filled out.

Why this works

Across 138 studies, monitoring progress toward a goal made people more likely to reach it, and the effect was larger when progress was physically written down or reported to someone.

4.3 Take your collected results weekly and compare it to your initial estimate from part 1, diagnose your gaps

  1. At your weekly checkpoint (Section 2.3), count how many sessions hit at least their minimum goal and divide by the sessions you planned. Write that completion rate down each week. One week alone is noise, so look at the trend over 3 to 4 weeks.
  2. Compare what you finished to the timeframe you set in Section 1.2. Are you ahead, on track, or behind?
  3. If you’re behind, name the cause and fix it in the matching section.
    CauseWhat it looks likeFix in
    EstimationGoals were bigger than they lookedSections 1.2 and 1.3
    SchedulingSessions got skipped or landed in low-energy slotsSection 2
    Focus or environmentSessions happened but little got doneSection 3
  4. A missed goal is not automatically a discipline problem. If you were sick, stressed, or your week blew up, say so. Diagnosing the wrong cause leads to the wrong fix.
Why this works

Trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of each day writing reflections scored higher on a final test than trainees who spent that time practicing.

4.4 Feed your revisions back into the plan and continue the cycle till the project is complete

  1. Make one change per week, the same way you run the experiments in Section 3.4. If you change everything at once, you won’t know what worked.
  2. Update your timeframe in Section 1.2 with what you now know about your real pace.
  3. Repeat: plan (Section 1), schedule (Section 2), work (Section 3), measure (Section 4), until the project is done.

Section 4 summary

Define a measurable target, log each session, and review progress weekly. Identify the cause of any gaps, adjust your estimates, and change one thing at a time.
